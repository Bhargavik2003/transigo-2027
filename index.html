01 · Overview

One network, two sides.

transigo is a connected logistics experience for the people who need goods moved and the transport partners who move them. The project asks one question: what changes if both sides see the same route?

Shipper side · research-backed

A shipper starts with a search — “Packers & Movers near me” — then collects two or three quotes by phone, WhatsApp and handwritten estimates. The price is guessed, negotiated and sometimes changed later. Choosing a mover comes down to trust or cost, with nothing to compare against.

What the interviews said

“I’m never sure if the driver is verified or trustworthy.”

“I wish everything was managed on one platform.”

Source: 21 interviews across shifters, business owners, transporters and drivers; service blueprint — booking & transparency.

Transport partner side · design hypothesis

On the other side, transporters and movers coordinate trucks, drivers and labour through contact lists, Excel sheets and phone calls. Vehicles are already assigned and moving, but that availability lives in logs and conversations that a shipper never sees.

The hypothesis behind transigo: if a vehicle’s route and a shipment’s route were visible in the same place, a match could be found rather than hunted for.

“Delays or cancellations by transportee/drivers. Overreliance on calls/WhatsApp for updates.”Movers & packers persona — frustrations

What is evidenced, what is proposed

Evidenced: manual records, WhatsApp-led coordination, difficulty finding workforce in peak season.

Proposed: route-level matching between available vehicles and open loads. This is a design proposal; it has not been tested with live data.

The response

transigo makes the route the shared object. A shipper builds a route and cargo; a transport partner sees loads on compatible routes and accepts one. Both then follow the same live route, the same documents and the same status.

Two workspaces share one system: a light workspace for shippers and a dark workspace for transport partners, joined by the route language in the design manual.

Where to see it

The connected service shows the seven-step route across both sides. The prototype lets you walk either one.

02 · Strategy

The coordination gap

Four beats of reasoning, each tied to something we heard or mapped. Scroll the story — the illustration changes with it.

01

Finding a transporter shouldn’t be a treasure hunt

The journey starts with a search engine and ends with a guess. Shifters search “Packers & Movers near me”, receive different quotes from two or three companies and choose on trust or cost. Estimates are calculated manually and often change.

“Final prices always change.”User quote · research
02

The right vehicle may already be moving

Transporters assign drivers and vehicles from Excel sheets, phone logs and contact lists. Their availability is real, but only visible to the people already in the conversation. A shipper can’t see it; a transporter can’t easily show it.

“I wish everything was managed on one platform.”User quote · research
03

Every handoff creates another coordination task

Between booking and delivery, a shipment passes through the transporter, driver, labour, checkpoints and the person waiting to receive it. Updates move by call and WhatsApp; proof moves by paper slip.

“Deliveries often get delayed without updates.”User quote · research
04

What if the network could connect the two?

If a shipment’s route and a vehicle’s route lived in one shared space, discovery could start from the route instead of the search. That is the design question the rest of this case study answers — for both sides.

46%

    Route Pulse: the route links origin, destination, vehicle and state. Motion explains a change; it never decorates. Try each principle.

    1 · Reveal

    A route line reveals or changes state when a journey begins or updates. 300 to 450 ms, ease in and out.

    2 · Move

    The vehicle marker slides along the route using Smart Animate with identical layer names; ETA, remaining distance and status update in the same transition.

    3 · Confirm

    Sheets and toasts fade in over about 200 ms. No celebration animation.

    Document added
    4 · One at a time

    Only the element that changed moves. No looping animation.

    5 · Reduced motion

    With reduced motion on, state changes cut instantly and the Route Pulse ring stays static. This page honours your system setting.

      08 · Reflection

      From scattered searches to connected movement.

      What I set out to do

      Make the route the shared object

      Research showed fragmented discovery on both sides. I used the route as the one thing a shipper and a transport partner could both see, and designed every screen around it.

      Decisions I’d defend

      Restraint, on purpose

      • Two workspaces, one system: light and dark, same components
      • Orange only for decisive commits
      • Motion only when it explains a change
      What isn’t proven

      Honest limits

      • Not usability-tested; no outcomes or adoption are claimed
      • Matching logic, prices and tracking are prototype data
      • Transport-partner side has no feedback or dispute flow yet
      What I’d do next

      Test the match

      • Usability-test the shipper request flow with real shifters
      • Test whether partners trust a suggested load
      • Prototype the feedback loop on both sides

      This case study describes a student concept. It documents research, design decisions and a prototype; it does not describe a launched product or measured business results.
